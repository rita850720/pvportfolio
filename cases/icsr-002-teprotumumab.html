Initial literature reportYoun et al., 2026 · PMID 41924363

A 70-year-old woman weighing 78.8 kg with Graves’ disease and thyroid eye disease, Hashimoto thyroiditis, hypertension, primary open-angle glaucoma, and baseline sensorineural hearing loss received teprotumumab infusions every three weeks beginning in March 2022. The publication did not report the trade name, dose, lot number, or exact infusion dates. Under the local infusion-center protocol, she received methylprednisolone 100 mg intravenously before each infusion. Her ongoing medications included levothyroxine, latanoprost, timolol, ophthalmic cyclosporine, and omeprazole.

Approximately 2.5 weeks after the first infusion, she developed nausea and vomiting. During the next six months of treatment, she experienced episodic intractable nausea and vomiting with muscle spasms. She had one emergency-department visit during the infusion course, two emergency-department visits during the following six months, and three hospitalizations for the gastrointestinal symptoms after treatment. Multiple antiemetics provided limited benefit, while tizanidine controlled the muscle spasms. Despite the events, teprotumumab was continued because her thyroid eye disease symptoms improved.

Her weight decreased by 41.2%, from 78.8 kg to a nadir of 46.3 kg. Serum lipase and amylase, electrocardiography, chest radiography, and abdominal/pelvic computed tomography were unrevealing. Endoscopy identified H. pylori; however, triple therapy did not resolve the symptoms. Thyroid-function and cortisol values varied. Tertiary adrenal insufficiency and corticosteroid-withdrawal syndrome were considered, but the planned ACTH stimulation test was not completed.

An empiric prednisone course was initiated and adjusted because symptoms recurred during tapering. Treatment was later changed to hydrocortisone, with short-term fludrocortisone that was stopped after hypokalemia. One month after corticosteroid initiation, the patient reported significant symptomatic improvement. She regained 21.2 kg during the following year and remained stable on hydrocortisone. The authors retained teprotumumab in the differential diagnosis but emphasized that concurrent corticosteroid exposure and the incomplete endocrine work-up prevented definitive attribution.

The case was considered serious because the nausea and vomiting required hospitalization. A reasonable causal possibility remained; however, dechallenge was not interpretable because teprotumumab was continued and improvement followed endocrine-directed treatment. Rechallenge was not separately reported.

Medical assessment

Documented reasoning

SeriousnessSerious due to inpatient hospitalization. “Severe” describes intensity; hospitalization establishes the regulatory seriousness classification.
ExpectednessNausea and weight decrease expected; vomiting and the prolonged, repeatedly hospitalized presentation potentially unexpected against the RSI used for review.
Reporter causalityTeprotumumab remained in the authors’ differential, but corticosteroid withdrawal/adrenal dysfunction, thyroid abnormalities, and H. pylori substantially confounded attribution.
DechallengeNot assessable: treatment continued, symptoms persisted after completion, and improvement followed corticosteroid replacement.
RechallengeNot separately reported.
Case dispositionValid serious initial literature ICSR; record event-level expectedness and complete duplicate reconciliation.

Follow-up

Open information requests

  • Confirm trade name, dose, number of infusions, lot number, and exact administration dates.
  • Obtain exact event-onset, emergency-department, hospitalization, and discharge dates.
  • Clarify whether vomiting and weight loss fully resolved and whether hydrocortisone remained necessary.
  • Obtain the planned ACTH stimulation test or final endocrine diagnosis, if completed later.
  • Clarify the action taken with each event and the clinician’s final causality assessment.
  • Perform duplicate search using patient, product, events, authors, institution, and publication details.

QC checkpoints

Source-to-case reconciliation

  • Trade name and dose not inferred from the active ingredient or standard regimen.
  • Seriousness based on hospitalization, not on the word “severe.”
  • Expectedness assessed separately for nausea, vomiting, and weight decrease.
  • Confounding corticosteroid exposure and incomplete endocrine work-up retained in the narrative.
